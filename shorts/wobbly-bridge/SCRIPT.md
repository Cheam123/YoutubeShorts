# "The Bridge That Taught London to Waddle" — script v1 (draft, awaiting approval)

Format: ~60 s vertical short, ~3.7 words/s, one steady narrator (ElevenLabs), word-by-word captions from Whisper timings.

## Facts and sources
- London Millennium Footbridge opened to the public 10 June 2000; about 90,000 people crossed on opening day
  (London Museum, "London's Millennium Bridge").
- It swayed sideways under the crowd and was closed two days later, on 12 June 2000 (Wikipedia, "Millennium Bridge, London").
- Cause: "synchronous lateral excitation" — walkers' sideways footfall forces; once the deck moves, people adjust their
  balance in time with it, which feeds the sway (Wikipedia; Arup's published analysis).
- Fix: 37 fluid-viscous dampers + 52 tuned mass dampers (89 total), about £5 million, work May 2001 – Jan 2002;
  reopened 22 February 2002 (Foster + Partners, "Millennium Bridge reopens"; Wikipedia).
- First new Thames crossing in central London since Tower Bridge (1894). Nicknamed the "Wobbly Bridge". Nobody was hurt.
- NOT used in the script because unverified this session: the 70 mm sway figure and "2,000 people on the deck at once".

## Script (225 words, ~61 s)
In the year two thousand, a brand new bridge made thousands of Londoners walk like penguins.
It's June. The Millennium Bridge is London's first new river crossing in over a hundred years, and ninety thousand people turn up to try it.
At first, it's perfect.
Then the deck slides a tiny bit to the left.
Nobody panics. They just do what you'd do on a rocking boat: spread their feet, and step with the sway.
Which pushes the bridge a little further.
So everybody steps a little wider. Which pushes it further still.
Soon, thousands of strangers are waddling side to side in perfect time, and not one of them agreed to it.
Luckily, the bridge was never going to fall down.
Unluckily, nobody standing on it knew that.
Two days after opening, London's newest bridge is closed.
You see, every step you take shoves the ground a little sideways. Left, right, left.
In a normal crowd, those shoves cancel each other out.
But once the deck starts moving, everybody's balance corrects the same way, at the same moment, and the cancelling stops.
Soldiers are ordered never to march in step across a bridge. Nobody here was marching. The bridge taught them how.
The fix took almost two years, five million pounds, and eighty-nine giant shock absorbers.
And Londoners still call it the Wobbly Bridge.
